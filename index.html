In Book 1, furor drives the action of the plot and sets the chain of events to follow the rest of the poem, however the poem has already decided the fate of piety to triumph. Virgil opens the first 4 lines of the Aeneid (1.4), “obliging brutal Juno’s unforgetting rage” which is intensified with the description of her rage being “deep-rooted”. Through Virgil’s opening, he mirrors the opening of Homer’s Iliad, with Achilles’ rage, and hence sets a tone of violence opening both of the epic poems. Virgil’s use of the adjective “unforgetting” signified her anger to be cruel and permanent and his reinforced with the use of a metaphor “deep-rooted” which illustrates and states her anger in comparison to a root, that won’t move, and her hatred will not stop. Gransden argues that most of the plot of the Aeneid is generated by Juno, and book one supports his ideals and argues correctly, it is Juno, “sister and consort of Jupiter’, whose rage that generates the violent storm (Book 1), that attacks Aeneas and his fleet into Carthage. However, it’s important to clarify, that generating the plot, isn’t the same as generating the plot forward, and if furor or piety pushes Aeneas to reach Italy. Therefore, Gransden’s argument shares the validity of Juno’s rage, however he doesn’t make the argument of the leading thread of piety throughout books 1, 2 and 4. Through his use of the hyperbole “hundred” and treating rage as a captive animal, he treats rage as a captor, and he states from book 1, rage and furor will be chained. This links the end of furor to Rome’s peace, where the closing of the gates of war, is equated with Augustus rule in 29 BC. Therefore, the argument furor itself drives the poem is insignificant, as if the destination of Aeneas’ is shown to be fixed from the start through piety, then piety and furor both creates the events of the Aeneid.
